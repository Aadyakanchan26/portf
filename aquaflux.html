AquaFlux

AquaFlux is a passive, vortex-driven drainage insert for surface drains. I designed it to help a drain capture water faster during intense rainfall, without electricity or moving parts.

Why I started

On a bus ride home, I watched rainwater pool around a drain even though the outlet was directly beneath it. The water had somewhere to go, so why could it not get there?

Surface-drain inlets can struggle to capture high-intensity rainfall quickly enough. Water can skim past an inlet or create backflow under pressure. I wanted to look at the inlet itself, not redesign an entire drainage system.

A waterlogged road: the problem that made me focus on the inlet.

How it works

AquaFlux fits into an existing surface drain inlet. Instead of relying only on gravity, its geometry creates a stable vortex. The vortex creates pressure and velocity gradients that pull water into the outlet, while the swirling flow helps resist debris buildup.

I treated the shape as the main variable: the system uses no power and no moving parts.

The four-stage development process began with field study.

CAD and optimisation

I used ANSYS simulations and mathematical modelling to compare more than 30 geometries. I tested different base shapes, vane angles from 20° to 65°, groove layering, and parallel arrangements.

ANSYS comparisons of geometry, vane angle, and design iterations.

Prototypes and water tests

I 3D-printed the optimised versions and tested them at different rainfall intensities and turbidity levels. The tests showed a 22% to 89% increase in discharge rate, exceeding the 15% benchmark I was working toward.

What is next

The government authority certified AquaFlux for deployment at both field sites this monsoon. I also built a surface-drainage algorithm that recommends an insert configuration from road conditions entered by a user.
