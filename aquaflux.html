Inspiration

On a bus ride home, I watched a rainwater pool around a drain, unable to reach the outlet sitting directly beneath it. The water had somewhere to go, so why couldn't it get there? Urban waterlogging is rampant, and as extreme rainfall and rapid urbanization intensify, surface drainage inlets can't capture water fast enough. Large volumes and high-intensity rainfall either skim past current inlets or cause backflow due to high pressure. The consequences are vast, from flooded roads, electrocution risks, delayed emergency services, and billions spent in annual losses, all traced back to an inefficient surface drainage inlet.

Vision

To design a surface drainage inlet that can increase drainage efficiency and handle high-intensity rainfall, with zero energy requirements, retrofittable without changing current plumbing infrastructure, and cheap enough to scale across every road.

System Overview

AquaFlux is a passive, vortex-driven drainage insert that fits into existing surface drain inlets and speeds water capture through geometry alone. Conventional drains rely only on gravity, so I turned to vortex hydrodynamics: a stable vortex creates natural pressure and velocity gradients that pull water in much faster, and its swirling flow keeps the inlet self-cleansing and resists debris buildup. My approach focused entirely on geometric optimization, asking how shape alone could produce a stable vortex and peak discharge. By bringing together fluid mechanics, computational fluid dynamics (CFD), hydraulic engineering, and design, the result needs no power or moving parts. It's also simple to produce: a single mould cast from recycled plastic keeps it cheap, sustainable, and easy to manufacture at scale.

Four step methodology.

1. Field Understanding

Worked with municipal engineers to conduct field studies at two waterlogging sites in my city to clearly define real-world drainage requirements. Measuring rainfall intensity, flood depth, inlet discharge, and catchment area per drain.

Those numbers became the targets the design had to hit, and they are what the calculations below are built on.

2. Design Optimisation

Using ANSYS simulations and mathematical modelling, I optimized the device by comparing 30+ geometries:

  • Selecting a cylindro-conical base geometry after comparing multiple shapes
  • Optimizing vane angles between 20° and 65°
  • Enhancing performance through groove layering and parallel set up

3. Prototyping & Testing

The optimized designs were 3D-printed and tested under varying rainfall intensities and turbidity levels. The results showed a 22% to 89% increase in discharge rate, exceeding the 15% efficiency benchmark required.

Comparing inlet against outlet flow, the parallel and groove-layered versions kept improving as inflow increased, which is exactly the behaviour you want in a heavier storm.

4. Validation & Deployment

The government authority certified AquaFlux for deployment at both sites this monsoon, with the adapted device and coupler ready for installation.

The adaptors are the part that came out of that last lab-record sketch: one for a parallel grate inlet and one for a perpendicular grate inlet, so the same device can go into either without touching the existing drain.

5. Surface drainage algorithm

Using the study's data, I built an algorithm that recommends the optimal insert configuration for any road condition entered, so AquaFlux can adapt to cities worldwide.

It lives at aquafluxvortex.com.

The digital calculator, which takes drain dimensions and drainage requirements and returns an adapted device configuration.

Impact

Utilise 120 kg of waste plastic per mile of road.

The key features and the engineering novelty as I summarise them: retrofits into any drain with an adjustable design, purely mechanical with no electricity needed, a 22–89% efficiency gain, about $5 per unit against a problem costing cities $20 billion a year, and a vortex design that accelerates discharge through geometry rather than by reducing flow.
