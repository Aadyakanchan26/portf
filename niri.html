Inspiration

It all began in the summer of 2023, when I was 13. I turned on the tap, and not a single drop came out. From my balcony, I watched tanker after tanker arrive; our society's borewells had dried up. There were endless discussions about solutions like rainwater harvesting and percolation pits.

But nothing changed.

This isn't just my story. Bengaluru faces a daily shortage of about 500 million liters of water, and globally, around 4 billion people live under high water stress.

Existing fixes kept stalling for the same three reasons: large infrastructure changes, high cost and complexity, and no one taking ownership. Every solution sat at the society or government level, and nothing reached the individual tap.

Five out of six borewells in our society had dried up, and we were getting water only through tankers. My city faced a terrible water crisis that summer, and the government even announced a penalty for using fresh water to clean cars. We were rationing water for 8 hours a day. It was so frustrating.

Many were oblivious to the situation and took water for granted; many were aware but did nothing to conserve it; they thought the government or society should provide water to meet their needs. I went deeper and made a survey to find out 3 key factors:

  • Lack of awareness of water consumption
  • Lack of any action to reduce water use at individual levels
  • Almost 40% of water is wasted because of irresponsible usage

Through my macro and micro surveys, I found something interesting: nearly half of household water loss comes from simple misuse, not scarcity.

We were mostly water illiterate, and also awareness and actions were very distant: North and South Pole. At that moment, I wanted to make water conservation simple, engaging, and intuitive like a daily habit. I wanted to make people aware that fresh water, the very source of sustaining life and business, is scarce and requires small steps from each individual to make a really big impact. And that started a water warrior campaign and a journey — of a device and system called NIRI, named after the Kannada word for water, Neeru.

Vision

Everyone should be able to save water every day, right at their tap. By pairing affordable technology with behavioral change, I want to make responsible water use a flexible, everyday habit that scales from homes to schools, hospitals, and public infrastructure.

System Overview

NIRI is a patented, IoT-enabled mechatronic water conservation system that cuts wastage through intelligent flow regulation, real-time leak detection, automatic shut-off, and behavior-driven nudges. It's built as a plug-in extension module that retrofits onto any existing tap or pipeline. Inside, a gear system drives a ball valve, rotating it to precise angles for fine-grained flow control. Refined over 6 iterations in 3 years, the device has 5 modes tuned to the highest-consumption points. It pairs with an AI water assistant that tracks consumption and uses the most effective methods to cut usage while maintaining comfort.

The five modes, as they were written down when the device was evaluated: Normal (tracks and displays real-time usage), Alert (notifies when consumption crosses a set threshold), Regulate (automatically reduces flow once limits are exceeded), Eco (temporarily halts flow to discourage excessive use) and Vacation (detects leaks and halts flow while nobody is home).

Journey

1. Building the Tech

I stepped into the project with just a question: how can individuals participate in water conservation, and how can technology change behaviour patterns and increase awareness? The idea seemed crisp and clear. The making was the opposite of that — messy, but still fun.

I had very limited knowledge of hardware and software at the time. My mentor at the ATL lab listened patiently and didn't demotivate me, and asked me to learn about the Arduino microcontroller. From there I began building NIRI. I learned the Arduino coding language, sent commands, and got the thing to do what I wanted. I practised soldering and sometimes slightly burnt my fingers. I wired everything up. Eventually the components fell into place.

I remember the first prototype I made was just an OLED display and a buzzer, and the wires ended up burning when I presented it in the most theatrical manner possible.

Over the iterations I adapted the power supply to be self-sufficient, with longer battery life for homes without a direct electricity connection. I made the controls more intuitive. And for the blocking mechanism I made a complete U-turn: it started with relays and a potentiometer and ended with a servo motor driving a gear mechanism on a ball valve. 3. Deploying and Piloting

The first pilot was my school canteen at Vidyashilp Academy. I was honestly anxious putting that first device in. I was afraid the pipe would burst from the pressure on the gears, or that the gears would buckle and block. I ended up checking on it every single hour, and my teachers definitely thought something was wrong with me that day.

From there, NIRI moved into hospitals and homes, where each site gave me different feedback. I am now installing with Century Builders, and office workspaces like Zerodha.

4. Validation

Funny story alert. I was on my sixth iteration, the device had been deployed and tested many times, and I walked into the government office with complete trust and confidence in it. As soon as I started my presentation I heard the low rumble of my motor — a sound I had heard many times before — and I immediately knew something was wrong. I had 15 government civil engineers staring at me while my motor chose to give up on the most important day, and I couldn't complete the demonstration.

I asked them for another day. I came back, demonstrated it properly, showed my results, and earned their technical validation for the device achieving up to 30% reduction. I now carry a spare set of every component in my device wherever I go. I call it the motor paranoia.
